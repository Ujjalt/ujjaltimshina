# The Monk and the Mask: A Reflection on Attention and Interpretation

*Bagdogra Airport | 3:41 PM, Thursday*

---

![Monk reading at the airport with a mask](https://github.com/Ujjalt/ujjaltimshina/blob/e350758f108afedea4e7c22d8eb2e05adf72fefa/images/Mask%20Monk.png?raw=true)

*The monk at Bagdogra Airport, captured in a moment of quiet contemplation.*

---

I noticed a monk sitting quietly at the airport, reading a book written in Tibetan. At first, he was without a mask, a mala resting in his hand, completely absorbed in what he was reading.

Then, from the corner of my eye, I noticed something.

People had begun staring at him. Not speaking to him. Not disturbing him. Just looking.

I watched him quietly reach into his bag, take out a mask, and put it on. He gathered his mala close to himself and, with a slight awkwardness, returned his attention to the book. I don't know why he put on the mask.

Perhaps it had nothing to do with the people around him. Perhaps he simply wanted to wear it at that moment. Or perhaps he had become conscious of all the eyes looking in his direction.

---

## I don't know.

And somehow, that uncertainty is what stayed with me.

Why did he feel the need to put on a mask just moments after so many people began staring? Was he uncomfortable? Did he feel exposed? Was the mask a way of creating a little distance between himself and everyone around him?

Or was I simply reading too much into a very ordinary moment?

I kept wondering about it.

He did not say anything. He did not react. He simply put on the mask and went back to his book, as though trying to disappear quietly into the world of whatever he was reading.

And I found myself thinking about how strange human attention can be. We look at someone because they appear different. They notice us looking. We may not intend any harm, yet somehow our curiosity can make another person conscious of being watched.

Perhaps that is what I witnessed.

Or perhaps it was something entirely different.

---

## I really don't know.

I only know that a few minutes later, the monk was still sitting there, still reading, still holding his mala, while I was sitting somewhere nearby wondering about the meaning of that small gesture.

I wrote in my phone at exactly 3:41 PM, Thursday, Bagdogra Airport:

> *"A monk was reading quietly. People started looking. He put on his mask and returned to his book."*

---


This small moment at the airport reminded me of something larger—how we often impose meaning on gestures we do not fully understand, much like how mathematicians once imposed suspicion on numbers they could not fully grasp.

> **Note:** This post revisits how long complex numbers lived in the shadows before mathematicians understood what they really were. Expressions involving √−1 appeared naturally while solving polynomial equations, yet for centuries they were treated with suspicion because they seemed to have no meaning within the existing number system. What makes this story interesting is not merely the eventual acceptance of complex numbers, but the long period of uncertainty that preceded it. Ideas that today are introduced in a standard undergraduate course were once considered strange, artificial, and even impossible. The development of complex numbers illustrates a recurring pattern in mathematics: new concepts often appear long before the language and intuition needed to understand them. Looking at this history reminds us that mathematical progress is rarely straightforward. Many of the concepts we now regard as fundamental emerged only after generations of debate, confusion, and persistence. In an age where attention is often focused on outcomes and achievements, it is worth remembering the intellectual struggles that shaped the mathematics we inherit today.

---


Just as the monk's gesture remained ambiguous—was it discomfort, self-consciousness, or simply a practical choice?—so too did the early interpretations of complex numbers remain uncertain for centuries. Were they real? Were they imaginary? Were they even valid mathematics?


I still think about that monk sometimes.

Not because I solved the mystery of why he put on the mask—I never did, and I likely never will. But because the uncertainty itself taught me something: that our interpretations of others are often more about us than about them.

And perhaps that is the deeper lesson.
