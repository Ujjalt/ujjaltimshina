  <h2 style="color:#e8e8e8; margin-bottom:8px;">The Silence of a Saturday</h2>
  <p style="color:#bfbfbf; font-style:italic;">
    August 1, 2026 • 11:08 AM
  </p>
</div>

<p>
There is something special about Saturdays in the mathematics lab.
</p>

<p>
Very few people come to the department on weekends. The corridors become quiet, the doors remain closed, and even the clocks seem to tick more slowly. I have always loved spending Saturdays and Sundays here. Not because I dislike people. Not because I hate my colleagues. Not because they distract me. I enjoy their company, and I learn a great deal from them.
</p>

<p>
I come because silence has a language of its own.
</p>

<p>
When I am alone in the lab, surrounded by books, papers, unfinished calculations, and a whiteboard carrying traces of yesterday's thoughts, I sometimes feel as though I am the only living creature left in the world. For a few hours, the race outside disappears. There is no comparison, no competition, no noise—only questions waiting patiently to be understood.
</p>

<p>
Today it is <strong>11:08 AM, Saturday, August 1.</strong>
</p>

<p>
Seventeen days ago, my supervisor gave me a mathematical problem. The truth is, I had already been wrestling with the same idea for months before that. Even now, I do not completely understand it.
</p>

<p>
I have written pages of calculations, erased countless mistakes, and followed arguments that eventually led nowhere. Sometimes I convince myself that I have finally understood the problem, only to realize that I misunderstood one small idea from the very beginning.
</p>

<p>
And strangely enough, this is what mathematics feels like.
</p>

<p>
People often imagine mathematics as certainty. But research mathematics is almost the opposite. We become confused. We develop misconceptions. We make mistakes, correct them, and then discover new mistakes hiding beneath the old ones. Progress is rarely a straight line. It bends, disappears, returns, and sometimes refuses to move at all.
</p>

<p>
The difficult part is that mathematics follows its own clock, while life follows another.
</p>

<p>
Deadlines continue. Expectations remain. Responsibilities never stop simply because an idea has not yet become clear. The race for survival does not wait for understanding. It simply moves forward.
</p>

<p>
Sometimes I wonder whether I am the only one who feels this way.
</p>

<p>
Then I speak to a few colleagues.
</p>

<p>
They smile and quietly say,
</p>

<blockquote style="
  border-left:4px solid #8ab4f8;
  margin:25px 0;
  padding-left:18px;
  color:#d8d8d8;
  font-style:italic;
">
"That happens to me too."
</blockquote>

<p>
Those simple words carry an unexpected comfort. They remind me that confusion is not the opposite of mathematics. It is often the place where mathematics begins.
</p>

<p>
Every researcher carries invisible struggles—unfinished proofs, abandoned ideas, questions that refuse to answer themselves, and moments of quiet doubt that no publication ever records.
</p>

<p>
Perhaps that is why I treasure these quiet Saturdays.
</p>

<p>
There is no audience here. No applause. No pressure to appear intelligent.
</p>

<p>
Just a mathematician, a stubborn problem, and the hope that one more page, one more calculation, or one more failed attempt might reveal a tiny piece of the truth.
</p>

<p>
And if it does not happen today,
I know I will come back next Saturday,
sit at the same desk,
open the same notebook,
and begin again.
</p>
